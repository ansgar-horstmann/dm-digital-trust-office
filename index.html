A guide for senior leaders

One backlog. One set of priorities. One owner per demand.

Demand management is how work reaches the Digital Trust Office — and how we decide what happens to it. Until now that decision lived in inboxes, chat threads and private spreadsheets. It now lives in one place, with a published set of rules. This page explains what the process fixes, why it should mean less work for you rather than more, and the seven things you need to do to start using it.

Prepared for domain and program leadership · Process owner: Ansgar Horstmann

  • 173 live and moving In prioritisation, discovery, business review, scheduling or development.
  • 66 deliberately parked Still valid, consciously deferred, each carrying a review date rather than silence.
  • 19 delivery teams in scope Each with a named lead who receives demands routed to them.

Question one

Which problems does this solve?

Six failures showed up again and again. None of them were caused by people doing a bad job — they were caused by there being no single place where demand was recorded and no agreed rules for handling it. Each row below is a real pattern from the current portfolio.

  1. 01

    There was no single backlog

    Before

    Requests arrived by email, chat, meeting aside and side spreadsheet. No one could answer the simplest question a leader can ask: what has actually been asked of my teams?

    Now

    Every demand is one record in one list, whatever channel it arrived through. The portfolio is countable, sortable and filterable to your area on demand.

  2. 02

    There was no way to park work

    Before

    The only available answers were yes and an awkward no. Work that was genuinely worth doing but not worth doing now had nowhere to sit, so it quietly evaporated — and reappeared six months later as an escalation.

    Now

    Deferred is a legitimate, recorded outcome with a review date attached. Sixty-six demands sit there today. Saying “not this quarter” is now a decision you can make and defend, not an omission.

  3. 03

    Ownership was assumed, never stated

    Before

    Items sat for weeks because every party assumed another party had picked them up. There was no moment at which ownership was explicitly accepted, so there was nothing to chase.

    Now

    Every demand carries a named demand owner and a named sponsor. If no one accepts ownership within fourteen days, the item escalates automatically — the process notices, so you don't have to.

  4. 04

    Cross-team dependencies were invisible

    Before

    When a demand touched three teams, one team heard about it. The other two discovered their involvement late, usually when a date was already committed.

    Now

    Each demand records a primary owning team plus any follow-up teams with a stake. Those teams are notified for awareness when the demand is routed, before dates get fixed.

  5. 05

    Priority was a matter of persuasion

    Before

    With no shared scoring basis, sequence was decided by whoever asked most persistently or most senior. Genuinely high-risk items could lose to well-argued convenience.

    Now

    One agreed scoring framework, taken from the firm's Digital Risk prioritisation criteria, replaces the argument — and regulatory obligations bypass it entirely as high priority by rule. Scoring switches on as the new intake questionnaire captures the nine inputs it needs; until then, sequencing is set by judgement at the monthly review.

  6. 06

    Nothing ever closed cleanly

    Before

    Delivered, cancelled and quietly abandoned all looked identical from the outside: an item that stopped being mentioned. That made both reporting and audit conversations painful.

    Now

    Closure is explicit and reasoned. Of the 502 demands on record, 153 are delivered and 110 are cancelled with a stated reason — and both remain in the record permanently.

In practice

Ownership clarity

PIPA — South Korea

Multiple legal teams — local and global — each believed they owned the PIPA (Personal Information Protection Act) compliance demand. With no single funnel, every team was partially aware, no one was fully accountable, and the requirement was stalling. Demand Management’s role: surface the overlap, assign clear ownership, and organise priority across all teams involved.

Demand aggregation

Data localisation — Australia & Japan

Contractual data-localisation requirements in Australia and Japan arrived as isolated, low-visibility asks — too small individually to drive a Platform McKinsey response. Collected through Demand Management as a single aggregated demand, they become a coherent, prioritised request with the weight to move the right teams.

Question two

Why this is less work, not more

A new process usually means a new form, a new meeting and a new report. This one is designed to remove all three. The effort that used to be spread across many people in an unstructured way is now carried once, by the process, in a structured way.

One front door

Requesters complete a single intake questionnaire that captures everything needed to score and route the demand. You stop fielding half-formed asks in chat and stop re-interviewing requesters for the detail that was missing.

Routing is proposed for you

Each new demand is read automatically and a proposed category and owning team come attached to it, with the reasoning stated. A person confirms or corrects that proposal. The triage meeting that used to exist to work out “whose is this?” is no longer needed.

Prioritisation happens centrally

One scoring framework will apply across all nineteen teams, so sequencing between two teams becomes a comparison rather than a negotiation. You inherit a ranked list instead of building one.

The process chases, you don't

Unowned items escalate automatically after fourteen days — running today. Once remediation clocks are live, items also get a reminder at three-quarters of their window. Nobody maintains a personal follow-up list.

Reporting is a by-product

A weekly summary and a monthly committee pack are generated from the same records the teams already maintain. Status reporting stops being a separate exercise performed the night before.

Closing work is legitimate

Because deferring and cancelling are defined states with reasons attached, teams can close things down cleanly instead of carrying a permanently growing list of maybes.

The honest total cost to you

Roughly fifteen minutes a week to read the digest and answer ownership requests, plus forty-five minutes a month for the portfolio review. Everything else is either automated or already part of how your teams work.

The vocabulary

The shared language you need to know

Most of the friction in demand conversations comes from words meaning different things to different people. There are only three sets of terms to be familiar with, and they are what the reports and the committee pack are built from.

Priority bands

Each demand scores up to 22 points across nine criteria — data sensitivity, asset criticality, firm-wide reach, client impact, regulatory obligation, strategic alignment, business impact, access requirements and product maturity. The score sets the band, and the band sets the clock.

Status: the framework and these thresholds are agreed, and the intake questionnaire has been built to collect all nine inputs. Scoring and the remediation clocks below begin operating once intake data is flowing — they are not yet reportable figures. Everything else described on this page is running today.

Priority bands and score ranges
Band Score What it means in practice
High 15 – 22 Sensitive data or a tier-one asset combined with material business impact. Anything with a regulatory obligation lands here automatically, whatever else it scores.
Medium 8 – 14 The majority of growth and improvement work. Tracked and reported, on a longer clock.
Low 0 – 7 Runs at team cadence. Visible in the backlog, not on a countdown.

The six things a demand can be

Category is decided once, early, and determines which track the demand follows. It is the single most useful field for a leader to understand, because it tells you whether something is on a clock or in a queue.

DEMAND
Issue management A live risk, control gap or regulatory matter. Reported to the committee; the 60- or 120-day remediation clock applies here. 33 today.
Team backlog Standard delivery work for a specific team’s plan. The most common case by a wide margin. 203 today.
External request Comes from outside the domain and needs coordination before anyone can own it. 16 today.
Resource request A staffing, budget or tooling ask rather than something to be built. Routed to portfolio review, not to a delivery team.
UCF input Introduces an additional control or requirement into the Unified Control Framework (UCF) or Continuous Control Verification (CCV) programme. Ensures new controls are formally registered and verified.
External demand A demand directed at a group outside DTO — requesting that another team, function, or partner implement, develop, or manage something in DTO’s interest. Demand Management clarifies priority and ensures the ask is formally tracked and followed up.

Parked versus stopped

This distinction did not exist before and is the one most worth carrying into your own conversations.

Deferred

Still a good idea. Deliberately not now. Carries a review date, stays visible in the portfolio, and comes back to you rather than being rediscovered by accident.

Cancelled

Decided against, with the reason recorded. Closed permanently but never deleted, so the decision can be explained months later.

Delivered

Done and in use. Stays on the record as evidence of what the domain has shipped.

Question three, first half

What is in it for you

The process asks a small amount of your attention. In return it is designed to remove five specific conversations that currently cost senior leaders far more time than fifteen minutes a week.

  • A defensible answer to “why isn't my thing being done?”

    Sequence comes from a published set of criteria applied identically to everyone. You can show the reasoning rather than absorb the pressure.

  • Protection for your teams' capacity

    Because parked work stays visible, “not this quarter” is a recorded decision instead of a broken promise. Your teams stop carrying invisible commitments.

  • No surprise escalations

    You see items ageing, unowned or approaching a deadline in the weekly digest — before they reach a committee agenda with your name next to them.

  • One portfolio view for your area

    Everything requested of your teams, its priority, its owner and its state, in one filtered view you can open before a conversation rather than commission after one.

  • An audit trail that already exists

    When internal audit or a regulator asks what was raised, what was decided and why, the answer is a filter rather than a reconstruction exercise.

Already running across multiple teams

The process is live across Kevin D’Costa’s full portfolio — which now includes Cross-Risk, Legal, Internal Risk, Conflicts, and the ServiceNow CoEI team. This demonstrates the workflow is practical and scales across teams before full automation is in place.

Question three, second half

What you need to do to onboard

Seven steps. The first two are one-off decisions your area makes once. Steps three and four cover how requests are submitted. Steps five through seven are the ongoing rhythm — a Slack reply, a weekly email, and one monthly call. There is no new system to learn.

  1. 1

    Nominate your Demand Owner

    One person from your team — typically a Director or Senior Director — becomes the named Demand Owner for all requests from your area. They are the single point of contact for triage, prioritisation decisions, and Slack confirmations.

  2. 2

    Identify a Sponsor for each request

    For each demand your team submits, name a business sponsor — the person from the requesting business side who owns the outcome and can confirm priority. This is not an internal DTO role; it is the stakeholder whose need the demand represents.

  3. 3

    Review the routing list

    Before submitting, check the routing list to see which DTO team covers your area. This takes 30 seconds and avoids mis-routed requests.

    View the routing list →

  4. 4

    Submit via the intake form

    All new demands go through a single form. Fill in the title, description, business need, and sponsor — that is all that is required to get started.

    Open the intake form →

  5. 5

    Reply to Slack assignment messages

    Once a demand is triaged, your Demand Owner will receive a Slack message asking to confirm ownership or provide context. A one-line reply is all that is needed — no meetings, no documents.

  6. 6

    Receive your weekly digest

    A short weekly digest email summarises the status of all active demands in your area — what moved, what is waiting, what is overdue. No action required unless something is flagged.

  7. 7

    Join the monthly portfolio review

    Once a month, active demands are reviewed as a portfolio. You will either receive a calendar invite or the review will be folded into an existing Monday call (to be confirmed). This is the forum for reprioritisation and escalation decisions.

On the automated suggestions

Categorisation and team routing are proposed automatically, with the reasoning written out in plain language next to the suggestion. A person confirms every one before it takes effect. The proposals are advisory by design, and the rate at which humans overrule them is tracked and reported — if the suggestions start drifting, that shows up in the monthly pack rather than quietly shaping the portfolio.

Questions we get asked

Before you ask

Is this another system I have to learn?

No. The process runs inside Jira and Slack. Requesters see a form; you see messages and a filtered list. There is no new tool to install, no licence to request and no training session.

Does software decide what my teams work on?

No. Scoring produces a consistent order and the automated categorisation produces a proposal. Both are inputs to a human decision, and the monthly review is where that decision is made. The value is that the discussion starts from a common baseline instead of from scratch.

What if something is genuinely urgent or regulatory?

Demands carrying a regulatory or compliance obligation are treated as high priority by rule, regardless of what they score elsewhere, and are flagged for committee reporting rather than queued. Urgency does not depend on your persuasiveness in the moment.

What happens to work I defer? Does it just disappear?

The opposite — that was the old failure mode. A deferred demand keeps its record and its review date and comes back to you at that date. It stays visible in your portfolio view the whole time, which is what makes deferring safe to do.

My area already has a working backlog. Why change?

Local backlogs work well within a team and poorly between teams. They cannot settle sequencing across two teams, they cannot show leadership a domain-wide picture, and they cannot answer an audit question about a demand raised two years ago. Your backlog keeps working — it is the layer above it that was missing.

Who owns this process, and who do I push back to?

Ansgar Horstmann owns the process, the scoring configuration and the reporting. Disagreements about a specific score or a routing decision go to him directly, and material changes to the framework are agreed with Risk & Compliance leadership rather than adjusted quietly.

How can I suggest improvements or make this even easier for my team?

This process is designed to evolve, and feedback from leaders is exactly how it improves. If something feels confusing, repetitive, or harder than it needs to be, that friction is worth raising — even a field you're unsure about or a step that feels redundant helps make the process sharper for everyone. Ansgar Horstmann is the point of contact for suggestions and would rather hear small friction early than see it quietly discourage adoption.
